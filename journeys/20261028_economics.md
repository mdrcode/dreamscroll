## Selected thread: economic history of American places

**Working question:** How did American places get built, hollowed out, and
rebuilt—and what does that explain about prices, wages, housing, and politics?

The corpus's American place-economics thread is not generic urban economics.
It is specifically about **circulation and loss**: hinterlands feeding cities,
downtowns concentrating capital, residential hotels housing the bottom of the
market, factories closing, and people migrating toward cheaper or recovering
places.

## Method: a place biography

Pick one place and trace five layers:

1. **Extraction and agriculture** — what hinterland did the place organize?
2. **Rail and industrial concentration** — what made it a production center?
3. **Downtown and housing** — how did the center and the bottom of the housing
   market evolve?
4. **Deindustrialization and labor** — what closed, and how did unions and
   firms distribute the adjustment?
5. **Migration and reindustrialization** — who stayed, who left, and what is
   the current recovery story?

Primary sources: Sanborn maps, historical census occupation maps, Brookings
migration data, BLS/Census employment data, local histories, and the corpus
captures above.

## Housing module: *Evicted*

Selected first book (2026-10-28).

Track six questions while reading:

1. **Causation** — where does eviction cause poverty rather than merely
   reflect it?
2. **Landlord economics** — small landlords, maintenance, vacancies, and the
   minimum viable property.
3. **Housing court** — what the legal process does to tenants who show up and
   those who do not.
4. **The housing ladder** — what the loss of SROs and cheap rooms did (Groth,
   #759), and how downtowns became office-only (Fogelson, #814).
5. **Policy** — vouchers, waiting lists, public housing, and why the private
   market fills the gap.
6. **Race, gender, children** — who bears the instability and how it compounds.

Companion after *Evicted*: Groth, *Living Downtown*, selectively; #344 on
housing costs and marriage; #128 on rent and creative work; #359 on modular
rowhouses; #507 on migration and affordability.
